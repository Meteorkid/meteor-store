# Apple CEO Tim Cook on Coming Out: Fifty-Three Before He Called It a Gift

> The most quoted line from Tim Cook's coming-out letter is the one about a gift. What stopped me was the half-sentence in front of it — "I have never denied my sexuality, I haven't publicly acknowledged it either, until now."

"The gift" is a cheap word now.

Open any platform and someone is telling you to be grateful for the things that hurt you: grateful for the wound, for the betrayal, for the father who never quite acknowledged you. The people saying it are usually standing somewhere safe.

So when I first read Cook's line — "I'm proud to be gay, and I consider being gay among the greatest gifts God has given me" — I was guarded. It was too complete. Too ready for a tote bag. An answer prepared in advance.

It was only after I finished the whole letter that I noticed a number: he was fifty-three when he wrote it.

## I

Before you can weigh a sentence, you need to know what kind of text it came from.

What *Bloomberg Businessweek* published on October 30, 2014, was not a feature written by a reporter. It was a signed essay by Cook himself, roughly eight hundred words. Not interviewed, not quoted, not interpreted — written by him, signed by him, with his name on the consequences.

There is something in a later interview that almost nobody quotes: he spent about a year getting the words to say what he wanted them to say, and choosing a moment that would be right for the company. The board supported it unanimously.

One year. Fifty-three years old.

Put those two numbers side by side and "gift" stops being a pretty phrase. It becomes a judgment a man bought with half a lifetime.

We are used to a fixed shape for stories like this: humiliated young, strikes back on the spot, rises. Cook's story does not follow that shape at all. He left this thing for last. First he spent sixteen years making a company nobody could replace, until he carried weight with both the board and the market — and only then turned to face a fact that had been with him since he was a boy.

There is no calculation in this, and he does not need anyone to defend him. By his own account the kids were the reason, not the leverage. But both things hold at once: he could bear the consequences, and he had run out of reasons to wait.

## II

Nor was the step he finally took a matter of "working it out."

By his account in two later interviews — with CNN and with *People en Español* — he had been receiving letters for years from young sexual minorities: bullied at school, thrown out of their homes by their own parents, some of them very close to suicide. He said he had always been an intensely private person, but that he had come to see staying closed off, under those circumstances, as a selfish thing to do.

That is paraphrase, not quotation. The original is "I started thinking that is a selfish thing to do." A small difference that changes the nature of the sentence: one is a verdict he passed on himself, the other a thought that surfaced slowly. The second is the one that sounds true.

Nowhere in the letter does he say his suffering was a gift.

The three things he does say are specific. That being gay gave him a deeper understanding of what it means to be in the minority, and a window onto what other minority groups face every day. That it gave him the skin of a rhinoceros, which comes in handy when you're the CEO of Apple. That it gave him the confidence to be himself and to follow his own path.

The gift was never the hardship. It was the hide that grew over it.

The distinction matters. The first version endorses the injury. The second concedes only this much: what I was handed was not up to me, but what I did with it is.

## III

I would rather read the letter as being about something else.

Not about sexual orientation, but about **how long it takes a person to stop apologizing for something they never chose**.

That something can be orientation. It can also be an accent, a height, the circumstances of a family, the part a parent could not give you, an old wound that refuses to close. Everyone's closet is different, but everyone has a door.

Cook took fifty-three years. That number calmed me down — not because "it's never too late," which is only a consolation, but because it settles one thing: there is no standard timetable. Some people say it clearly at twenty. Some never say it at all. Neither is a failure. The dividing line is not age. It is whether you still believe you owe someone a briefing.

The phrase "coming out" carries a ceremonial feel, as though it were a single settlement after which everything is settled. In practice it is more like delivery in instalments: tell one person today, another next year, and some people never need to know. That is not cowardice. That is proportion.

## IV

The last paragraph of the letter is, I think, where the word "gift" finally earns itself — and it is the least quoted part.

He writes that when he arrives in his office each morning, he is greeted by framed photographs of Dr. King and Robert F. Kennedy. He writes that he does not pretend writing this puts him in their league; all it does is allow him to look at those pictures and know that he is doing his part, however small.

There is no rhetoric in that paragraph. It records one action: a man sits down each morning, looks at two faces on the wall, and starts work.

For many years before that, he had probably looked at those two faces every morning with something in between — the knowledge that by certain standards he did not measure up. That is not the world judging him. That is him judging himself. And that kind of trial is harder to dismantle than any outside prejudice, because it requires no witness.

Writing the letter did not turn him into someone else. It only let him see those two photographs, at last, as company rather than as an exam.

So what was the gift?

Not the thing he did not choose. The day he stopped apologizing for it.

It came late. But it came. And for anyone still waiting for that day, knowing that somebody else waited and arrived is already a little light.

## Appendix: The Letter

Published by *Bloomberg Businessweek* on October 30, 2014, signed by Cook. Reproduced in full and unabridged.

The letter is here in full because both claims this essay makes can be checked against it on the spot: that he never once calls his suffering a gift, and that the lightest thing in the whole piece — the thing that comes closest to "gift" — is the paragraph about two photographs on his office wall.

**Tim Cook Speaks Up**

Throughout my professional life, I've tried to maintain a basic level of privacy. I come from humble roots, and I don't seek to draw attention to myself. Apple is already one of the most closely watched companies in the world, and I like keeping the focus on our products and the incredible things our customers achieve with them.

At the same time, I believe deeply in the words of Dr. Martin Luther King, who said: "Life's most persistent and urgent question is, 'What are you doing for others?'" I often challenge myself with that question, and I've come to realize that my desire for personal privacy has been holding me back from doing something more important. That's what has led me to today.

For years, I've been open with many people about my sexual orientation. Plenty of colleagues at Apple know I'm gay, and it doesn't seem to make a difference in the way they treat me. Of course, I've had the good fortune to work at a company that loves creativity and innovation and knows it can only flourish when you embrace people's differences. Not everyone is so lucky.

While I have never denied my sexuality, I haven't publicly acknowledged it either, until now. So let me be clear: I'm proud to be gay, and I consider being gay among the greatest gifts God has given me.

Being gay has given me a deeper understanding of what it means to be in the minority and provided a window into the challenges that people in other minority groups deal with every day. It's made me more empathetic, which has led to a richer life. It's been tough and uncomfortable at times, but it has given me the confidence to be myself, to follow my own path, and to rise above adversity and bigotry. It's also given me the skin of a rhinoceros, which comes in handy when you're the CEO of Apple.

The world has changed so much since I was a kid. America is moving toward marriage equality, and the public figures who have bravely come out have helped change perceptions and made our culture more tolerant. Still, there are laws on the books in a majority of states that allow employers to fire people based solely on their sexual orientation. There are many places where landlords can evict tenants for being gay, or where we can be barred from visiting sick partners and sharing in their legacies. Countless people, particularly kids, face fear and abuse every day because of their sexual orientation.

I don't consider myself an activist, but I realize how much I've benefited from the sacrifice of others. So if hearing that the CEO of Apple is gay can help someone struggling to come to terms with who he or she is, or bring comfort to anyone who feels alone, or inspire people to insist on their equality, then it's worth the trade-off with my own privacy.

I'll admit that this wasn't an easy choice. Privacy remains important to me, and I'd like to hold on to a small amount of it. I've made Apple my life's work, and I will continue to spend virtually all of my waking time focused on being the best CEO I can be. That's what our employees deserve — and our customers, developers, shareholders, and supplier partners deserve it, too.

Part of social progress is understanding that a person is not defined only by one's sexuality, race, or gender. I'm an engineer, an uncle, a nature lover, a fitness nut, a son of the South, a sports fanatic, and many other things. I hope that people will respect my desire to focus on the things I'm best suited for and the work that brings me joy.

The company I am so fortunate to lead has long advocated for human rights and equality for all. We've taken a strong stand in support of a workplace equality bill before Congress, just as we stood for marriage equality in our home state of California. And we spoke up in Arizona when that state's legislature passed a discriminatory bill targeting the gay community. We'll continue to fight for our values, and I believe that any CEO of this incredible company, regardless of race, gender, or sexual orientation, would do the same.

And I will personally continue to advocate for equality for all people until my toes point up.

When I arrive in my office each morning, I'm greeted by framed photos of Dr. King and Robert F. Kennedy. I don't pretend that writing this puts me in their league. All it does is allow me to look at those pictures and know that I'm doing my part, however small, to help others.

*— First published in Bloomberg Businessweek, October 30, 2014.*
