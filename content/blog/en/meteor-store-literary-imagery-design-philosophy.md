Late at night, this shop feels most like itself.

You open the page late at night. The screen is charcoal-black, but not empty. A distant glow rests behind the glass edges. A few stars hold their ground. Somewhere above them, a meteor passes before you can decide whether you truly saw it.

Nothing asks you to stop and admire it. Navigation stays navigation, prices stay prices, and the night simply breathes around the useful things.

I built Meteor Store on nights like these.

Not with a team or investors, but with one computer, one pair of hands, and more late hours than I can count. Those hours became a language: the little shop, the handwritten note, the meteor, the fixed star, and the trail joining one moment to another. Later came the Twenty-Eight Mansions, the Four Symbols, the Northern Dipper, the Seven Luminaries, and a lunar cycle beneath a monthly plan.

They were never meant to turn a software shop into a museum of ancient astronomy. I wanted a modern place whose structure remembers an older way of reading direction and time.

## A Little Shop Lit After Midnight

I call this place a shop on purpose.

“Platform” would sound larger, but it does not describe the relationship I want with the people who arrive here.

A shop has a keeper.

Its keeper’s hands remain visible in what is sold. There is a counter, a lamp, a letter beneath it, and a terminal glowing in the back room. When a stranger pauses at the window, another person is on the other side.

That is why the site returns to tools and handiwork, milk tea and tuition, and letters written after closing time. Each product began with a problem that had already cost me time, patience, or money. An order carries an obligation to deliver something real. The smallness keeps the story honest.

The light in this shop is not meant to announce an empire. It says only: someone is still here, making things.

## Every Meteor Carries My Name

“Meteor” was not a celestial label added after the fact. In Chinese, my real name sounds like “meteor shower.”

That made the meteor more than a convenient logo. It became the most personal mark in the whole visual language: a signature moving across the dark.

It appears in the shop’s identity, beneath an article, and in a joke told while something loads. Double-click an empty space and a few small meteors appear; reach the bottom and a brief streak crosses the screen. One-time meteor confetti after payment is my thank-you to a stranger.

I think of the meteor as action: brief, but directional. Fixed stars are the works that remain after release. A star trail is the handwriting of time—one release, correction, and encounter slowly becoming a road.

This language is still growing. A rocket survives in an older corner, while the full-screen meteor-shower burst and interactive wishing star have been implemented but are not yet part of the live experience. I would rather admit that the design is still being edited than call an unseen flourish finished.

A meteor crosses the sky for only a few seconds, yet I have always felt that it burned in earnest.

## Night Is Not a Background Color

The darkness here is fixed. It does not turn white when the operating system changes its theme.

That choice is partly autobiographical: most of this place was made after midnight, with the computer the brightest object left awake. But the dark is also physical. Glass needs something behind it to refract; violet light needs shadow; a meteor needs night.

So the site uses charcoal rather than absolute black, with restrained violet and rose light, sparse stars, occasional aurora-like colour, and a little grain to keep the digital surface from becoming sterile.

Glass belongs to navigation, overlays, controls, and interactive cards. Long essays stay on solid dark ground. The words must remain more important than the window around them.

The motion follows a similar rhythm: slow environment, quick feedback, rare celebration.

Background light may breathe and a distant star may drift, but a button should answer immediately. A successful action may earn one small ceremony, never an endless performance.

## Then I Left a Star for Every Essay

For a long time, a post was one line in a list: title, date, summary. Efficient, familiar—and unlike how writing feels to me. An essay is a point where someone stopped, looked carefully, and left a light behind.

So each article became a star in its own right.

Each article’s date places it in time, and together the articles form a chronological trail. Sections and tags keep their ordinary work outside the map. If companion stars come later, they should represent real relationships, not decorative lines.

Twenty-eight fine marks surround the map in four groups of seven, recalling the Twenty-Eight Mansions and Four Symbols without becoming twenty-eight new filters. The article remains what you select; the cultural coordinates are quiet enough to discover or ignore.

The [National Astronomical Observatories of China](https://www.nao.cas.cn/ncb/gz/202204/t20220406_6419883.html) notes that traditional Chinese sky maps relied largely on stars, connecting lines, and written names. That restraint inspired me: relationships, rather than mythical creatures, create the order.

Here, the Mansions offer direction, not divination. On hover or focus, a badge explains this shop’s literary choice: Kui for writing, or the Heart Mansion as the Azure Dragon’s heart. Green-blue, vermilion, silver-white, and blue-black trim the badges without replacing section colours. They are an interpretive palette chosen for this shop, not a claim that tradition comes with canonical RGB values.

The essay remains the star. Culture supplies a bearing by which it may be found.

## Reading the Sky, Keeping Time, Shaping Experience into Words

The phrase I return to is **观象、授时、成章**—a movement from reading the sky, through keeping time, to shaping experience into words.

The [Institute for the History of Natural Sciences](https://english.ihns.ac.cn/an/an/201310/t20131010_110579.html) describes sky-gazing and season-granting as two central themes of ancient Chinese astronomy. My use of them is not reconstruction, but design translation.

To read the sky is to give content direction through the Mansions and Four Symbols. To keep time is to let dates leave traces in the Northern Dipper’s seasonal inscription, a Seven Luminaries timeline whose mapping still needs correction, and “one lunar cycle” beneath the monthly plan. To shape experience into words is to let works rest as stars and time and relationship become their trails, until scattered essays form a night sky that can be read.

Pricing receives only a small measure of this language. Monthly, annual, and lifetime remain explicit; beneath them sit one lunar cycle, one turn of the Dipper, and a light that does not go out. Term, price, duration, and entitlement stay in front.

That is the principle behind the whole system: Chinese in structure, contemporary on the surface; function first, imagery half a step behind.

## To Look Up Is Also to Learn the Names

A cultural reference becomes hollow when it is used only because it sounds old or beautiful. A recent review found places where this shop had not named the sky carefully enough.

The clearest example is **斗宿**, the Dipper Mansion. It is the first Mansion of the northern Black Tortoise; its six principal stars are the Southern Dipper in Sagittarius, distinct from the seven-star Northern Dipper. The [Beijing Planetarium](https://www.bjp.org.cn/xwzx/gndt/4028c1368c64c42a018c68c73057001f.shtml) makes that distinction clear. I had used the Northern Dipper’s seasonal handle to explain this Mansion. That misidentification needs correction: the Mansion should return to its context, while the seasonal inscription belongs to the Northern Dipper.

The Seven Luminaries need the same discipline. Used as weekdays, they should derive from an article’s date and cycle through Sun, Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, and Saturn. At present, the labels still follow each post’s position in the list, while the underlying sequence is Sun, Moon, Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn. Until that is corrected, they should not be mistaken for a date-based calendar, much less a live planetary display.

The outer marks are equally spaced for visual rhythm. The [Palace Museum](https://www.dpm.org.cn/lemmas/244773.html) describes the Mansions as an ancient division around the celestial equator and ecliptic, used to mark the Moon’s movement. This shop has not plotted each Mansion’s measured span. Four equal groups of seven are neither a precise historical chart nor a live sky.

The seasonal inscriptions also use four fixed Gregorian month-and-day boundaries. They are stylized four-season notes, not calculations of exact solar terms or astronomical ephemerides.

The broader vocabulary is still settling too: a meteor marks the header, while a rocket remains in the footer. I would rather admit that than pretend the language has always been perfectly unified.

Respect for tradition does not begin by adding more ancient names. It begins by learning what the names mean, and by correcting them when I have used them carelessly.

## Let Poetry Step Half a Pace Back

The most important design decision is often knowing when the metaphor should become quiet.

A visitor must see monthly, annual, and lifetime before moon, Dipper, and everlasting light. Prices, access, payment, refunds, security messages, and errors must be plain. No one should interpret a constellation to understand a purchase.

Cultural details should not create another click, modal, or maze of filters. Long-form text stays off glass. On mobile, the pricing cards retain one slender vertical trail while particles, blur, and density become lighter.

Accessibility belongs inside the story. Motion must stop when requested; transparency must recede. The same information should remain available in high-contrast modes, from a keyboard, and through a screen reader.

Performance asks for the same humility: stable star positions, capped pixel density, lighter mobile effects, and atmosphere that never delays the task.

If a piece of romance can be reached only by someone who can see it, tolerate its motion, and afford a powerful device, then it is not yet complete design.

Once the path is clear, starlight no longer has to carry more than light.

## Starlight Must Eventually Reach the Ground

The sky is where this shop keeps its metaphors. The ground is where it must keep its promises.

The products here grew out of pitfalls I had already encountered. I wanted to turn those hard-won lessons into a smoother road for someone else. Pathfinder is a path, not a prophecy. The opposite of feeling lost is not certainty, but action.

Action is less elegant than a constellation: fixing a bug, answering a message, delivering a licence, maintaining old work, or admitting a cultural mistake.

Once the payment meteor fades, there is still an order to fulfil—and the payment helps cover another small part of my tuition. Celebration is brief because responsibility is not.

The student plan comes from the same place. I once thought mostly about turning past pitfalls into an easier road; now I hope to offer the umbrella earlier—before the next person is soaked by the same rain.

That is where the rain and the night sky finally meet.

A star can offer direction, but it cannot walk the road. Metaphor cannot replace a useful tool, fair terms, careful maintenance, or a human answer.

By the time you close the page, the meteor is gone.

The little shop is still here.

A lamp remains on. The terminal is running. Somewhere in the dark, another small thing is being tested and corrected.

I think of works as stars, time as the trails between them, and essays as Mansions in which a thought may briefly rest. But decoration has never been what truly lights this sky. The light comes from one person having acted in earnest—and another person, because of it, having a shorter stretch of difficult road to walk.
